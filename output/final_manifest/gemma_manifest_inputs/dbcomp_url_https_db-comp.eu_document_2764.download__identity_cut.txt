Whereas:

1. INTRODUCTION
(1) This Decision is addressed to Google LLC (formerly Google Inc.) and to Alphabet
Inc. (“Alphabet”), both incorporated in the United States of America (“USA”),
(together referred to as “Google”). It concerns a series of practices by Google related
to online display advertising intermediation services.
(2) This Decision establishes that a series of business practices of Google in the online
display advertising intermediation markets constitute a single and continuous
infringement of Article 102 of the TFEU and of Article 54 of the EEA Agreement
(“the Infringement”).5
(3) This Decision also establishes that Google’s buy-side conduct, on the one hand, and
sell-side conduct, on the other hand, as described below, constitute, each by
themselves, an infringement of Article 102 TFEU and of Article 54 of the EEA
Agreement.
(4) In particular, the Commission considers that Google is or has been:
(1) abusing its dominant position in the EEA-wide market for programmatic ad
buying tools for the open web within the meaning of Article 102 TFEU and
Article 54 of the EEA Agreement by engaging in a variety of practices aimed
at favouring its supply-side platform (“SSP”) which results in the potential
exclusion of rivals and in the strengthening of its position in the EEA-wide
market for SSPs for online display advertising (“Buy-side Conduct”), and
(2) abusing its dominant position in the EEA-wide market for publisher ad servers
for online display advertising within the meaning of Article 102 TFEU and
Article 54 of the EEA Agreement by engaging in a variety of practices aimed
at favouring its SSP which results in the potential exclusion of rivals and in the
strengthening of its position in the EEA-wide market for SSPs for online
display advertising (“Sell-side Conduct”).
(5) This Decision is structured as follows. Section 2 provides an overview of Google’s
and the complainants’ activities. Section 3 summarises the procedure relating to the
proceedings in this case. Section 4 describes the main elements of the adtech industry
and Google’s activities in that industry. Sections 5 to 7 explain the Commission’s
conclusions regarding the relevant product and geographic markets and Google’s
dominant position in those markets. Section 8 contains the Commission’s analysis of
Google’s abuses of that dominant position. Section 9 sets out the Commission’s
jurisdiction. Section 10 describes the effect of the infringements on trade between
Member States. Section 11 identifies the addressees of this Decision. Finally, Section
12 outlines the remedies imposed by this Decision, explains the method for
calculating the fine and sets the amount of the fine imposed.

5 References in the Decision to Article 102 TFEU, to effect on trade between Member States or to
competition within the internal market are to be taken to include Article 54 of the EEA Agreement,
effect on trade between Contracting Parties to the EEA Agreement, and competition within the territory
covered by the EEA Agreement.

3.2.1. The French NCA Decision of 7 June 2021
(50) In 2019, the Autorité de la Concurrence, the NCA of France (hereinafter the “French
NCA”) started investigating certain aspects of Google’s practices involving the ‘sellside’ of its ad tech business. In the context of that investigation, on 30 September
2020, the French NCA issued a statement of objections with preliminary findings

(51) Google applied to benefit from a settlement procedure as provided for in the French
Commercial Code.15 This procedure allows for a settlement, fixing the minimum and
maximum amount of the financial penalty that could be imposed in a final decision,
if the undertaking does not contest the reality of the objections brought against it.
The undertaking may, in addition, choose to undertake to change its behaviour to put
an end to the authority’s competition concerns, which can be taken into consideration
in relation to the fine to be imposed. Google thus undertook not to dispute the
findings of the French NCA and proposed commitments to improve the
interoperability of its publisher ad server and SSP with third-party publisher ad
servers and SSPs. The French NCA granted Google’s request to benefit from this
settlement procedure.
(52) On 7 June 2021, the French NCA issued a decision (hereinafter the “French NCA
Adtech Decision”), which fined Google EUR 220 million. The French NCA Adtech
Decision found that from 1 January 2014 until 30 September 2020 (the date of
adoption of the French NCA’s statement of objections), Google had abused its
dominant position in the market for ad servers for publishers of websites and mobile
apps, in violation of Articles L. 420-2 of the French Commercial Code (Code de

4.1. Description of the industry
(117) This Decision concerns online display advertising intermediation. Online advertising
comprises two broad categories: online search advertising and online display
advertising.84 As regards online display advertising, ads can be purchased either

to as “walled gardens”, “owned and operated” or “O&O” channels). Google is an
O&O platform as regards the inventory on its proprietary services such as Gmail,
Google Finance, and YouTube. Other players active in the O&O channel include
Meta with its social networks and Amazon with its marketplace.85

(118) In the case of ads purchased through the open display channel, when a user opens a
website or an app, a complex series of transactions - nearly instantaneous and
invisible to the user - determines which ad will be shown to that user in each
available ad space on the webpage/app. The set of technological tools that connect
website/app publishers selling advertising opportunities to the advertisers wishing to
buy those advertising opportunities is referred to as the “adtech stack".
(119) Each website/app can be programmed by its publisher to create slots where ads can
be displayed. A non-search related ad displayed on a website/app is called a (online)
display ad. A display ad may contain images, text, or multimedia. A single display ad
shown to a single user on a single occasion is called an impression.
Figure 2: Adtech stack87

(120) An adtech transaction begins when a user opens a website/app. While the
website/app’s content loads, the website/app typically sends a request to a publisher
ad server to select which ads will fill each ad slot on the page. The publisher ad
server evaluates potential ads from different advertising sources and applies a
decision-making logic to determine which ad will be displayed to the user opening
the website/app.
(121) Publisher ad servers are thus the first technological layer involved in the selection
and distribution of online display advertising.
(122) For a typical website/app, the publisher ad server first determines whether the ad
spaces on the webpage/app opened by the user have already been sold to a specific
advertiser directly by the publisher. Such direct sales result from one-on-one
negotiations between publishers and advertisers and typically involve premium ad
placements (e.g., ads at the top of a webpage) that command the highest prices from
advertisers.88 For any ad space not filled through direct sales, the publisher ad server
then tries to sell the ad space through indirect sales channels (“programmatic sales
via the open web”).89 Programmatic sales via the open web allow publishers to sell
remaining or “remnant” ad space (i.e., space not sold through direct sales). Many
publishers, especially smaller ones, only sell ad space through this channel.

(123) Publisher ad servers have four main features, namely (i) they allow the
implementation, delivery and follow-up of ad campaigns sold directly to advertisers;
(ii) they enable programmatic sales via the open web; (iii) they make it possible to
decide on the most profitable ad to display, taking into account both direct and
indirect sales, for each impression; and (iv) they enable monitoring of sales
performance. Publisher ad servers generally charge the publisher a fee based on the
number of impressions served. The publisher ad server fee typically does not vary
according to the price paid for each impression.
(124) In the case of programmatic sales via the open web, a series of interactions between
different adtech tools follows. These technologies allow publishers and advertisers to
transact through instantaneous automated processes, known as programmatic buying.
The importance of programmatic buying has increased significantly in recent years.
The defining feature of programmatic buying is that the decision on whether to buy a
particular impression is made in real time, making use of information about the
environment (for example the webpage) in which the ad will appear and often about
the internet user to whom the ad will be shown.
(125) Historically, the first programmatic intermediaries were the publisher-facing ad
networks (“publisher ad networks”), whose model consisted of grouping together the
spaces available on the websites of multiple publishers, often according to criteria
such as their exposure or the theme of the websites, and marketing them to
advertisers, paying the publishers a fixed price. For smaller publishers, i.e. primarily
those who do not have a direct relationship with advertisers (and are often not
equipped with an ad server), publisher ad networks have represented an ideal
solution.
(126) Today, most open web programmatic transactions take place on an ad exchange (also
called a supply-side platform or SSP)90 and publisher ad networks have become
gradually less relevant. On the one hand, since SSPs offer more extensive control
options (in particular with regard to the content of the campaigns displayed), they
became the preferred option especially of larger publishers.91 On the other hand,
publishers ad servers, unlike publisher ad networks, have offered publishers the
possibility to call multiple SSPs when accessing advertiser demand.92 As a result,
most publisher ad networks have now changed their model or no longer exist.
However, there is no consensus on the definition of publisher ad networks and the
term is still sometimes used to designate certain actors, in particular those offering
limited control to publishers over their inventories.93
(127) An SSP is a platform used by publishers, typically through requests received from
their publisher ad server to auction ad impressions on a particular webpage/app. The
SSP solicits bids on the impression from ad buying tools, chooses the winning bid,

of the transaction amount, i.e. of the price received by the publisher in exchange for

(129) When a publisher ad server sends an auction request to an SSP, the publisher ad
server provides certain information about the impression for sale. This can include
information about the website/app itself, the ad space on the webpage/app (e.g.,
where the ad is placed) and the user who will view the impression. The data about
the user may include the demographic characteristics of the user (e.g. location, age,
gender) and insights as to a user’s interests, based on past browsing behaviour.
(130) After receiving this information from the publisher ad server, the SSP may
supplement the information with any additional information the SSP might
independently have about the user viewing the ad. For this to happen the SSP needs
to be able to match the user identity (User ID) received from the publisher ad server
with its own identifier of that user.
(131) The SSP then transmits the bid request, along with any information gathered about
the user and the website/app, to various ad buying tools. The detailed information
concerning the user’s location and browsing history is highly valuable to advertisers
because it helps them to assess the value of a particular impression to its advertising
campaign. For example, if the information tells an advertiser running a retail
business that the user had previously browsed its website/app but did not complete a
purchase, then that retailer may be willing to pay a premium for the particular
impression.
(132) Advertisers receive and respond to bid requests using programmatic ad buying tools
(also referred to as “ad buying tools” or “buy-side tools”). These programmatic ad
buying tools, which include DSPs or advertiser-facing ad networks (“ad networks”),
assist advertisers with connecting to SSPs, selecting impressions to bid on,
submitting bids, and tracking the purchased impressions against the advertiser’s
advertising campaign goals. The programmatic ad buying tools also try to identify
the user by matching their own identifier for that user and the one received from the
SSP. Advertisers also use advertiser ad servers, which are used notably to store the
“creative” (i.e. the video or text content that is shown to the user) and track the
performance of the ad campaigns.95 Advertiser ad servers are not relevant for any of
the conducts assessed in this Decision.
(133) DSPs provide sophisticated and customizable services that allow advertisers to
manage their advertising purchases. The modalities for managing DSP bidding vary
across a broad spectrum, with, at one end, services that optimise bidding strategies

programmatic ad buying tool then sends it back to the SSP for consideration.

determine the winning bidder. Historically, most SSPs ran a second-price auction in

bid of the second highest bidder. Today, however, most SSPs run first-price auctions
where the highest bidder simply pays the price of its winning bid. The SSP sends the
information about its winning bid back to the publisher ad server, which evaluates
the SSP’s bid under a set of rules defined by the publisher ad server.
(137) The publisher ad server then makes the final decision regarding which ad to “serve”
to the user. For each impression, a publisher ad server usually sends a request for a
bid to multiple SSPs and selects the best bid that these different SSPs are able to
submit, often in an auction. After making its selection, the publisher ad server sends
a message to the programmatic ad buying tool representing the winning advertiser.
The advertiser ad server then provides the content of the ad to be displayed.

referred to as “buy-side” services, to buy ad inventory. The adtech transaction

5.1. Principles relating to product market definition
(144) In the context of the application of Article 102 TFEU, the definition of the relevant
product and geographic market is essential to assess whether the undertaking
concerned has a dominant position and whether this enables it to prevent effective
competition from being maintained on the relevant market by giving it the power to
behave to an appreciable extent independently of its competitors, its customers and,
ultimately, consumers.104
(145) The concept of the relevant market implies that there can be effective competition
between the products or services which form part of it and this presupposes that there
is a sufficient degree of interchangeability between all the products or services
forming part of the same market in so far as a specific use of such products or
services is concerned.105

5.4.4. Ad servers are not substitutes to programmatic ad buying tools for the open web
(213) In its previous decisional practice, the Commission, despite leaving the precise
product market definition open, considered the supply of publisher ad server services
and the supply of advertiser ad server services to be in different markets to the supply
of programmatic ad buying tools for the open web to the extent that “the various
services provided in the ad tech value chain, such as DSP, SSP, ad exchange and ad
server services, are all based on distinct technologies that pose unique challenges
and serve a specific purpose.”205
(214) For the reasons set out below, the Commission finds that the market for
programmatic ad buying tools for the open web does not include publisher ad servers
or advertiser ad servers.
(215) On the buy-side, advertiser ad servers are used by advertisers and media agencies to
store the ads, deliver them to publishers, and keep track of this activity. In addition to
hosting the creative content, the main activities performed by an advertiser ad server
can be divided into reporting206 and media management207. However, they do not
perform the main function of programmatic ad buying tools, which is to purchase
online display advertising space for advertisers and media agencies. This is also
confirmed by the NCA reports.208
(216) On the sell-side, publisher ad servers manage publishers’ inventory and are
responsible for the final choice of which ad to serve, based on the bids received from
different SSPs and the direct deals agreed between the publisher and advertisers.
While programmatic ad buying tools provide services to advertisers on the “buy
side” of the adtech stack, publisher ad servers mainly perform the publisher sales
function, on the “sell-side” of the adtech stack.209
